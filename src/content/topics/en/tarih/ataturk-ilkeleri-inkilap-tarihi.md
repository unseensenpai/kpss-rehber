**Official scope:** Turkish history from the collapse of the Ottoman Empire to the end of the Second World War.

## Core idea

Think of this topic in three steps: **organising national resistance to occupation** (1918–1920), **military and diplomatic victory** (1920–1923) and **building a modern state** (1923–1938). The backbone of the questions is what each document decided. You need to know which document says that *the independence of the nation will be saved by the nation's own resolve*, and what that statement means.

## From Mudros to the congresses (1918–1919)

- **Armistice of Mudros (30 October 1918):** especially **Article 7**, which let the Entente occupy any place that threatened its security, opened the way to occupation.
- Societies for and against the national cause were founded; local armed resistance became known as **Kuvâ-yı Milliye**.
- **19 May 1919:** Mustafa Kemal landed at Samsun.
- **Amasya Circular (22 June 1919):** declared that the country's integrity and the nation's independence were in danger and that the nation's own resolve would save its independence. The first sign of a new state based on national sovereignty.
- **Erzurum Congress (23 July – 7 August 1919):** regional in how it met, national in its decisions. Mandates and protectorates were rejected.
- **Sivas Congress (4–11 September 1919):** national in every respect. The societies were merged into the **Association for the Defence of the Rights of Anatolia and Rumelia**; a mandate was rejected for good.
- **National Pact (Misak-ı Millî, 28 January 1920):** the document of national borders and full independence, accepted by the last Ottoman Chamber of Deputies.

## The Assembly and the War of Independence (1920–1922)

- After the **official occupation of Istanbul on 16 March 1920** and the dissolution of the Chamber of Deputies, the **Grand National Assembly (TBMM) opened on 23 April 1920**. It adopted government by assembly and put down internal revolts.
- **Treaty of Sèvres (10 August 1920):** partitioned the empire; it never came into force and the Assembly did not recognise it.
- **Eastern Front:** the **Treaty of Gyumri (Gümrü)** with the Armenians (3 December 1920), the Assembly's first international treaty.
- **Western Front:** after the **First Battle of İnönü** (January 1921), the **Teşkilat-ı Esasiye** (20 January 1921), the first constitution. The **Second Battle of İnönü** (March–April 1921), defeat at **Kütahya-Eskişehir**, the **Commander-in-Chief Law** and the **national requisition orders** (August 1921). The **Battle of Sakarya** (23 August – 13 September 1921) turned defence into offence.
- **Southern Front:** the **Treaty of Ankara** with France (20 October 1921): an Entente power recognised the Assembly.
- The **Treaty of Moscow (16 March 1921)** and the **Treaty of Kars (13 October 1921)** set the eastern border.
- **Great Offensive (26 August 1922)** and the **Battle of Dumlupınar (30 August 1922)**; then the **Armistice of Mudanya (11 October 1922)** recovered Istanbul and Eastern Thrace without fighting.

## Lausanne and the Republic

- **Abolition of the sultanate (1 November 1922).**
- **Treaty of Lausanne (24 July 1923):** the new Turkish state's independence was recognised internationally and the capitulations were abolished. Some issues were left open, such as Mosul, the administration of the Straits and Hatay.
- **Ankara becomes the capital (13 October 1923)**, **proclamation of the Republic (29 October 1923)**, **abolition of the caliphate (3 March 1924)**.

## Atatürk's reforms

- **Political:** abolition of the sultanate and the caliphate, the Republic, the 1924 Constitution, attempts at multi-party politics (Progressive Republican Party 1924, Free Republican Party 1930).
- **Law:** the **Turkish Civil Code (1926)** put family law and women's rights on a secular basis.
- **Education and culture:** **Unification of Education (Tevhid-i Tedrisat, 3 March 1924)**, the **Alphabet Reform (1 November 1928)**, the Nation's Schools, the Turkish Historical Society (1931) and the Turkish Language Association (1932), the 1933 University Reform.
- **Social life:** the Hat Law (1925), closure of dervish lodges (1925), the international calendar and clock (1925–1926), units of measurement (1931), the **Surname Law (1934)**, women's right to vote and stand in local (1930) and parliamentary (1934) elections.
- **Economy:** the **İzmir Economic Congress (1923)**, abolition of the tithe (*aşar*, 1925), the Cabotage Law (1926), and after the 1929 crisis **statism** and the **First Five-Year Industrial Plan** (1934).

## Atatürk's principles

**Republicanism, nationalism, populism, statism, secularism and revolutionism (reformism).** The six principles entered the **constitution in 1937**. A typical question matches a reform to a principle: the Surname Law with populism (ending privileges), the Unification of Education with secularism and nationalism, the proclamation of the Republic with republicanism.

## Foreign policy in Atatürk's era

- Issues left from Lausanne: **Mosul** (left to Iraq by the 1926 Treaty of Ankara), the **population exchange**, **foreign schools**.
- **Joining the League of Nations (1932)**, the **Balkan Pact (1934)**, the **Saadabad Pact (1937)**.
- **Montreux Convention (1936):** control of the Straits passed to Turkey.
- **Hatay** became an independent state in 1938 and joined Turkey in 1939.
- Turkey stayed out of the Second World War; in **February 1945** it declared war on Germany and Japan so that it could be a founding member of the United Nations.

## Commonly confused

- The *Amasya Circular* is a revolutionary document; *Erzurum* was regional in how it met, *Sivas* was national in every way.
- *Gyumri* was the Assembly's first international success; the *Treaty of Ankara (1921)* was its first treaty with an Entente power.
- The *Teşkilat-ı Esasiye (1921)* is the Republic's first constitution; the *Kanun-i Esasi (1876)* is the Ottoman Empire's first.

## Study tip

Work through 1918–1923 on a dated timeline and group the reforms by area (political, law, education, society, economy). For each document, keep a one-sentence note on what happened for the first time.
