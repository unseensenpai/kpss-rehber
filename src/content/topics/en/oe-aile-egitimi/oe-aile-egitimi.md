## Core idea

The most constant teacher of a child with special educational needs is the family. Family education (*aile eğitimi*) gives families **information, skills and support** so that what is learned at school continues at home and the family's quality of life improves. There are **2 questions**; they ask about family reactions, programme types and how the teacher communicates with families.

## Family and diagnosis

- **Reactions after diagnosis:** feelings such as shock, denial, guilt, anger, sadness and, over time, acceptance may appear. These feelings are **not lived in the same order by every family** and can resurface at times.
- **Family needs:** information, social support, financial and emotional support, access to community services; identified through interviews and scales.
- **Siblings:** siblings also need information and support.

## Family education programmes

- **Individual training:** working with one family according to its priorities.
- **Group training:** families with similar needs come together for information and sharing experience.
- **Home visits:** teaching takes place in the child's natural setting and daily routines.
- **Parent-mediated instruction:** parents learn techniques such as modelling, prompting and reinforcement and use them at home.
- **Support groups:** settings where families give each other emotional support.

## Communication and collaboration

- The family is an equal partner in the **IEP** process and takes part in decisions.
- The teacher **listens without judging**, uses clear language without heavy jargon and respects confidentiality.
- Regular communication (a home-school notebook, meetings) keeps home and school consistent.

## Commonly confused

- In the *family-centred* approach the family sets priorities; in the *expert-centred* approach the specialist does.
- A *support group* focuses on emotional sharing; a *family education programme* focuses on building knowledge and skills.

## Study tip

Eliminate options where the teacher blames the family, leaves them out of decisions or dismisses their feelings; the right answer is usually the one that treats the family as a partner.
