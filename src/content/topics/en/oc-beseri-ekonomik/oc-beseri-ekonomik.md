## Core idea

Human and economic geography explains people's **distribution**, **movements** and **economic activities** on the Earth through natural and human factors. The basic question is always the same: "Why is this **here**, and why at **this intensity**?" The test has 20 questions; they usually ask about reading graphs and tables, distribution factors and theories.

## Population and migration

- **Population density:** **arithmetic** (population / total area), **physiological** (population / farmland), **agricultural** (farming population / farmland).
- **Factors in distribution:** natural factors such as climate, relief, water and soil, and human factors such as industry, transport, history and policy.
- **Demographic transition model:** from high births and high deaths, through falling deaths and then falling births, to low births and low deaths.
- **Population pyramid:** a wide base shows a young, fast-growing population; a narrow base an ageing one. The **dependent population** is children and the elderly outside working age.
- **Migration:** internal or international, voluntary or forced, permanent or seasonal; **brain drain** is the migration of skilled workers. Migration is explained by **push** factors (unemployment, war, disaster) and **pull** factors (jobs, education, safety).

## Settlement

- **Rural settlements** may be nucleated or dispersed; dispersal relates to rugged relief, scattered water and scattered farmland.
- **Urbanisation:** industrialisation and farm mechanisation speed up rural-urban migration; a **metropolis** is a large city that shapes its surroundings, and linked metropolises form a **megalopolis**.
- **Central place theory (Christaller):** settlements form a hierarchy by the services they offer; higher-order services are found in fewer, larger centres.
- **Urban functions:** industry, trade, port, administration, tourism, education.

## Economic activities

- **Sectors:** **primary** (farming, livestock, mining, forestry, fishing), **secondary** (industry), **tertiary** (services), **quaternary** (information and research).
- **Farming:** **extensive** farming uses low inputs over large areas with low yields; **intensive** farming uses high inputs on limited land with high yields; a **plantation** grows a single export crop in the tropics.
- **Von Thünen model:** as distance from the market grows, land rent falls and the type of farming changes in rings.
- **Industrial location factors:** raw materials, energy, labour, capital, market, transport. Weight-losing raw materials (sugar beet) are processed near the source, perishable goods near the market. **Weber** looks for the point of lowest transport cost.
- **Energy:** **non-renewable** (coal, oil, natural gas, nuclear) and **renewable** (hydropower, wind, solar, geothermal, biomass).
- **Transport and trade:** sea transport is the cheapest over long distances; canals and straits are strategic chokepoints.

## Development and globalisation

- **Indicators:** income per person, the **Human Development Index** (income, education and health), employment by sector, urbanisation rate.
- **Globalisation:** multinational companies and flows of capital and information; growing economic and cultural interaction.
- **Cultural geography:** the distribution of religions and language families, cultural diffusion and culture regions.

## Commonly confused

- *Arithmetic density* uses total area; *physiological density* uses farmland.
- *Extensive* farming means large areas and low yields; *intensive* farming means heavy inputs and high yields.
- A *metropolis* is one large city; a *megalopolis* is a belt of merged metropolises.

## Study tip

Learn each theory as a drawing (von Thünen's rings, Christaller's hexagons, the demographic transition curve). For graph and diagram questions it is enough to lay these drawings over the question in your mind.
