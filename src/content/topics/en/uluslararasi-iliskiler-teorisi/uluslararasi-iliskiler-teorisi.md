## Core idea

International relations theory tries to explain why states go to war, why they cooperate and how the international system works. Each theory picks a different **main actor** and **level of analysis** and gives a different meaning to **anarchy**. The test has **8 questions** on this subject; they usually ask you to match a view with its theory or thinker, or to compare the assumptions of two theories.

## The discipline and the great debates

- **Birth of the discipline:** it was institutionalised after the First World War, with the aim of preventing war.
- **First debate:** between *idealism* (peace through international law and organisations) and *realism*. E. H. Carr criticised idealism in *The Twenty Years' Crisis*.
- **Second debate:** between *traditionalists* and *behaviouralists*, who defended scientific method.
- **Later debates:** the inter-paradigm debate and the *positivism–post-positivism* (rationalist–reflectivist) debate. Sources number the debates differently.

## Realism and liberalism

- **Classical realism:** the state is the main, rational actor; human nature seeks power. Morgenthau defines national interest in terms of **power**.
- **Neorealism (structural realism):** for Waltz, behaviour is shaped not by human nature but by the **anarchic structure** and the distribution of power. States focus on **relative gains**.
- **Defensive and offensive realism:** Waltz argues that states seek security, Mearsheimer that they seek to **maximise** power.
- **Security dilemma:** one state's arming for its security makes others feel less secure.
- **Liberalism:** cooperation is possible; trade, democracy and institutions support peace. The **democratic peace** thesis holds that democracies do not fight each other.
- **Neoliberal institutionalism:** known for Keohane and Nye's **complex interdependence**; institutions and **regimes** reduce cheating, and states look at **absolute gains**.

## Critical and alternative approaches

- **Marxist approaches:** the **dependency school** stresses core–periphery inequality; Wallerstein's **world-systems** analysis divides the world into core, semi-periphery and periphery.
- **Constructivism:** for Wendt, anarchy is not given; states' **identities and norms** shape it. Interests are socially constructed.
- **English School:** Bull's **international society**; despite anarchy, shared rules and institutions exist.
- **Critical theory:** for Cox, theory is always for someone and for some purpose; it questions the existing order.
- **Feminist and post-structuralist approaches:** highlight gender and the link between discourse and power.

## Key concepts

- **Levels of analysis:** individual, state and international system (Waltz's three images).
- **Hegemonic stability:** an open economic order is stable when a hegemonic power sustains it.
- **Balance of power:** other states balancing against one that becomes too strong.

## Commonly confused

- *Classical realism* looks for the causes of war in human nature, *neorealism* in the structure of the system.
- *Relative gains* are the realist emphasis, *absolute gains* the neoliberal one.
- *Constructivism* stresses identities and norms; the *English School* stresses the society states form and its shared rules.

## Study tip

Make a table for each theory: main actor, view of anarchy, whether cooperation is possible, key thinker. When a question gives a sentence and asks which approach it belongs to, the table takes you straight to the answer.
