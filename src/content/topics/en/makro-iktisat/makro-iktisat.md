## Core idea

Macroeconomics studies the economy **as a whole**: total output, the general price level, unemployment, and the fiscal and monetary policy that affect them. The central debate is whether markets reach **full employment** on their own; the Classics say "yes", Keynes says "not always". The test has **10 questions**. They focus on multiplier calculations, shifts in IS-LM graphs and the policy advice of the schools.

## National income accounting

- **GDP** (*GSYH*): the market value of final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a period. By the expenditure method, **Y = C + I + G + NX**.
- **GNP** (*GSMH*) is based on nationality: GNP = GDP + net factor income from abroad.
- **Nominal** GDP uses current prices, **real** GDP constant prices. **GDP deflator** = nominal / real × 100.
- Intermediate goods and transfer payments (such as pensions) are **not** counted in GDP; double counting is avoided.

## The Classical model and the Keynesian system

- **Classical model:** **Say's law**, flexible prices and wages; the economy is at full employment on its own. Money is **neutral**: by the quantity theory MV = PY, money supply affects only prices.
- **Keynes:** wages are **sticky** downwards; **effective demand** determines output, so an underemployment equilibrium is possible.
- **Consumption function:** C = a + bY; b is the **marginal propensity to consume** (MPC), 1 − b the marginal propensity to save (MPS).
- **Multipliers (closed economy, lump-sum tax):** spending multiplier 1 / (1 − MPC); tax multiplier −MPC / (1 − MPC); the **balanced budget multiplier** is 1. In an open economy the propensity to import makes the multiplier smaller.
- **Paradox of thrift:** if everyone tries to save more, income falls and total saving may not rise.
- **Accelerator principle:** investment depends on the **change** in income, not its **level**.

## IS-LM and AD-AS

- **IS:** goods-market equilibrium; downward-sloping. Higher government spending or a tax cut shifts IS **right**.
- **LM:** money-market equilibrium; upward-sloping. A higher money supply shifts LM **right**.
- **Expansionary fiscal policy** raises income and the interest rate; the higher rate reduces private investment (**crowding out**).
- **Extreme cases:** if LM is **vertical** (the classical case), fiscal policy is ineffective and monetary policy fully effective. If LM is **horizontal** (**liquidity trap**), monetary policy is ineffective and fiscal policy fully effective.
- **AD-AS:** aggregate demand slopes down. Short-run supply slopes up; long-run supply is **vertical** at potential output. Supply shocks (such as energy prices) can cause **stagflation**.

## Inflation and unemployment

- **Types of inflation:** **demand-pull** (excess aggregate demand) and **cost-push** (input prices, wage increases).
- **Types of unemployment:** **frictional** (job search), **structural** (skills mismatch), **cyclical** (falling demand). Frictional plus structural is the **natural rate of unemployment**.
- **Phillips curve:** in the short run inflation and unemployment are inversely related. According to Friedman and Phelps it is **vertical in the long run** (the expectations-augmented Phillips curve).
- **Okun's law:** the inverse link between a rise in unemployment and real output falling below potential.

## Schools

- **Monetarists:** money supply should follow a rule; fine-tuning creates instability.
- **New Classicals:** **rational expectations** and flexible prices; anticipated policy has no real effect (**policy ineffectiveness**).
- **Real business cycle theory:** fluctuations come from real shocks such as technology.
- **New Keynesians:** accept rational expectations but explain price and wage **rigidities** with micro foundations (menu costs).
- **Supply-side economics:** tax cuts raise incentives to work and invest; the **Laffer curve**.

## Commonly confused

- *GDP* is based on geographic borders, *GNP* on nationality.
- In a *liquidity trap* monetary policy is ineffective; in the *classical case* fiscal policy is.
- *Demand-pull* inflation comes with rising output; *cost-push* inflation with falling output.

## Study tip

Draw IS-LM on paper and apply each policy one by one: which curve shifts, in which direction, and what happens to the interest rate and income. Make these four steps a habit and you will solve graph questions without memorising.
