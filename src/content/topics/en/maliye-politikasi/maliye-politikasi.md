## Core idea

Fiscal policy (*maliye politikası*) is the state's use of **taxes, spending and borrowing** for economic goals. The main goals are price stability, full employment, growth, fair income distribution and external balance. The basic logic is to raise total demand in a recession and reduce it when the economy overheats. This subject has 6 questions in the test; they ask you to choose the right policy for a situation, interpret multipliers and compare schools of thought.

## Tools and direction

- **Expansionary policy:** raising public spending or cutting taxes; used in recession and unemployment.
- **Contractionary policy:** cutting spending or raising taxes; used against demand-pull inflation.
- **Automatic stabilisers:** smooth the business cycle without new decisions; a **progressive income tax** and **unemployment insurance** are typical examples.
- **Discretionary policy:** deliberate tax and spending changes decided by the government.
- **Structural budget balance:** the balance with cyclical effects removed; it shows the true stance of policy. **Fiscal rules** set numerical limits on deficits or debt.

## Multipliers and lags

- **Spending multiplier:** a rise in public spending raises income by more, depending on the marginal propensity to consume; in the simple model it is 1/(1−MPC).
- **Tax multiplier:** the effect of a tax change is **smaller** in absolute value than the spending multiplier, because part of a tax cut is saved.
- **Balanced budget multiplier:** if spending and taxes rise by the same amount, income rises by that amount in the simple model; the multiplier is **1** (Haavelmo).
- **Inside lags:** recognising the problem and deciding; **outside lag:** the decision taking effect. In fiscal policy the inside lag is usually long because of the legislative process.

## Schools of thought

- **Classical school:** markets clear by themselves; the state should be neutral and the budget balanced.
- **Keynesian school:** when effective demand is insufficient, fiscal policy works; deficits can revive demand.
- **Monetarists:** fiscal policy has limited effect because of **crowding out**; the money supply matters more.
- **Rational expectations:** anticipated policies do not affect real variables; **Ricardian equivalence** also suggests debt-financed expansion may have no effect.
- **Supply-side economics:** argues that tax cuts raise output by encouraging work, saving and investment.

## IS-LM and the open economy

- In a **liquidity trap** (horizontal LM), interest rates do not rise, there is no crowding out and fiscal policy is very effective.
- With a **vertical LM** (classical case), crowding out is complete and fiscal policy does not raise income.
- **Mundell-Fleming model:** with perfect capital mobility, fiscal policy is **effective under fixed exchange rates** and **ineffective under flexible rates**.

## Commonly confused

- *Automatic stabilisers* work by themselves; *discretionary policy* needs a government decision.
- The *inside lag* is time spent deciding; the *outside lag* is time until the effect appears.
- The *spending multiplier* is larger than the *tax multiplier*, which is why even a balanced budget raises income.

## Study tip

For each scenario, first identify the problem (recession or inflation), then choose the direction, and finally add the school or model condition (crowding out, exchange-rate regime). Work through the multiplier formulas once yourself with a simple numerical example.
