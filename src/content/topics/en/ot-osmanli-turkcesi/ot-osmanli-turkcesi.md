## Core idea

Ottoman Turkish is a written language using the Arabic alphabet **from right to left** and combining Turkish, Arabic and Persian elements. For a history teacher the goal is to be able to read archival documents and inscriptions. The test has 3 questions; they usually ask you to read a short text, to recognise a word's pattern or a compound, and about document types.

## Alphabet and spelling

- Most letters take different shapes at the **beginning, middle and end** of a word.
- **Non-connecting letters:** elif, dal, zel, ra, ze, je and vav do not join the letter after them.
- **Vowel letters** (*imla harfleri*): in Turkish words they show the vowels; **elif** (a, e), **vav** (o, ö, u, ü), **ye** (ı, i), **he** (a, e at the end of a word).
- **Harakat** (*hareke*): üstün, esre and ötre mark short vowels in Arabic words and are usually left out in texts.
- **Nasal kef** (*sağır kef*): stands for the Turkish nasal "ñ" sound.

## Arabic and Persian elements

- **Wazn (pattern):** Arabic words are built from a root and a pattern; the *fâil* pattern shows the doer (kâtib, writer), the *mef'ûl* pattern the thing done (mektûb, letter).
- **Plurals:** sound plurals with *-ât* and *-în*; **broken plurals** by changing the pattern (kütüb, books).
- **Persian izafet:** built with the linking *-ı/-i*, the head noun coming first (Bâb-ı Âlî, the Sublime Porte).
- **Persian affixes:** *bî-* and *nâ-* make negatives, *-hâne* shows a place, *-dâr* possession.

## Scripts and documents

- **Naskh** (*nesih*) is the book hand, **rika** the quick everyday hand, **divani** the chancery hand used in decrees and warrants, and **siyakat** the script of financial records.
- A **ferman** is a sultan's order, a **berat** a warrant of appointment or privilege, an **arz** a petition to a superior; **tahrir defterleri** are population and tax registers and **şer'iyye sicilleri** are the court records of the kadı (judge).

## Commonly confused

- A *ferman* is an order; a *berat* is a document granting someone authority or a privilege.
- *Divani* is formal and ornate; *rika* is plain and everyday.
- The *active participle* (*ism-i fâil*) shows the doer; the *passive participle* (*ism-i mef'ûl*) shows what is affected.

## Study tip

Read a few lines of rika every day to get used to the joined letter forms. Spotting patterns and vowel letters helps most in recognising a word.
