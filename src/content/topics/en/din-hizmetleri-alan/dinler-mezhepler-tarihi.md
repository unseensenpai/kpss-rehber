## Core idea

This course presents religions and schools **as they understand themselves**, in a neutral and descriptive way. In the history of religions, know each religion's founder or prophet, scripture, core belief and place of worship. In the history of Islamic schools (*mezhep*), the key is knowing **which question** each school arose to answer: the caliphate, the grave sinner, the relation of faith and deeds, the balance of reason and revelation. Questions mostly ask you to match a view with the right school. Depth varies by level: bachelor's candidates follow the İlahiyat faculty curriculum, associate candidates the İlahiyat associate programme and secondary-level candidates the İmam Hatip curriculum.

## Classification of religions

- **By source:** **revealed** (divine) religions and religions of human origin.
- **By spread:** **universal** religions addressing all humanity (Christianity, Buddhism, Islam) and **national** religions tied to one people (Judaism, Hinduism, Shinto).
- **By founder:** religions with a known founder (Buddhism) and those without a single founder (Hinduism).

## Major religions

- **Judaism:** based on the Torah revealed to Moses. The scripture is the **Tanakh** (Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim); the **Talmud** is the written form of the oral tradition's interpretation. The place of worship is the synagogue, and the weekly holy day is the **Sabbath**.
- **Christianity:** rooted in Jesus. The scripture is the Old and New Testaments; the four Gospels are Matthew, Mark, Luke and John. The core belief is the **Trinity**. Doctrines were defined at **church councils** (the first at Nicaea, 325). The main branches are Catholicism, Orthodoxy (the 1054 schism) and Protestantism, which began with Luther.
- **Hinduism:** the scriptures are the **Vedas** and Upanishads. **Karma** (the consequence of action), **samsara** (the cycle of rebirth) and **moksha** (liberation from the cycle) are central; society is organised by the caste system.
- **Buddhism:** founded by Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha). It teaches the **Four Noble Truths** and the **Eightfold Path**; the goal is **nirvana**. The main branches are Theravada and Mahayana.
- **Others:** Confucianism and Taoism (China), Shinto (Japan), Jainism and Sikhism (India), Zoroastrianism (Iran).

## How schools emerged in Islam

- **Political causes:** the question of the caliphate; the martyrdom of Uthman, the battles of the Camel and Siffin and the **arbitration** (*tahkim*) produced the first divisions.
- **Intellectual causes:** different understandings of verses and hadith; the balance of reason and revelation.
- **Social and cultural causes:** Muslims from different cultures and encounters with other religions and philosophies.
- Schools differ on the **interpretation** of the religion, not on its essence.

## Theological and legal schools

- **Kharijites (*Haricilik*):** arose from those who rejected the arbitration. They regard the grave sinner as having left the faith and do not tie the caliphate to any lineage. The branch that survives today is the **Ibadiyya**.
- **Shia (*Şia*):** holds that the **imamate** (*imamet*) of Ali and his descendants was set by explicit text (*nas*). The main branches are the Twelvers (*İsna Aşeriyye*, Ja'fari), the Zaydis and the Ismailis.
- **Murji'a (*Mürcie*):** keeps deeds separate from faith and leaves the grave sinner's fate to God (*irca*, postponement).
- **Mu'tazila (*Mutezile*):** traced to Wasil ibn Ata. It holds that the grave sinner is in a position between faith and unbelief (*el-menzile beyne'l-menzileteyn*). It has five principles (*usul-i hamse*) and gives reason a wide role.
- **Ahl al-Sunna (*Ehl-i Sünnet*):** **Maturidism** (Abu Mansur al-Maturidi, Samarkand) gives reason a wider role and is linked with the Hanafis; **Ash'arism** (Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari) follows a middle way between reason and revelation and spread among Shafi'is and Malikis; **Salafism** (the line of Ahmad ibn Hanbal) stresses adherence to the texts and the way of the first generations.
- **Schools of law:** Hanafi (Abu Hanifa), Maliki (Malik ibn Anas), Shafi'i (al-Shafi'i), Hanbali (Ahmad ibn Hanbal) and the Shia Ja'fari school.

## Commonly confused

- The *Kharijites* consider the grave sinner an unbeliever, the *Mu'tazila* place him between two positions, the *Murji'a* leave the judgement to God.
- *Maturidism* and *Ash'arism* are theological schools; *Hanafi* and *Shafi'i* are schools of law.
- The *Tanakh* is Judaism's scripture; the *Talmud* is its tradition of interpretation and commentary.

## Study tip

Build a table writing each school's view on the grave sinner, faith and deeds, and the caliphate in one line. For religions, memorise the trio of founder, scripture and key concept; most questions come from these matchings.
